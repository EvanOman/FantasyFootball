04B

Early ranks carry more value. There is no magic cutoff.

Ten completed seasons, 2016–2025. This tests the value of collecting top-ranked players and gives rank gaps a points-based scale.

79.4%of positive value came from the eventual top 30
58.8%was captured by the preseason top 40
2.0×average value of preseason ranks 1–10 versus 31–40

Stars matter much more after ordinary production is subtracted. Here, replacement is the next unselected player at each position after filling all 64 offensive starting slots across an eight-team superflex league. A player’s positive value is max(0, season PPR points − replacement points), divided by 16 or 17 scheduled games. Injuries and zero-production seasons stay in the data.

The preseason top ten returned 16.1 total PPR points per game-equivalent and 4.6 above replacement, on average. Ranks 31–40 returned 12.9 and 2.3. The first ten beat the next ten in 7 of ten seasons. Ranks 51–60 actually beat 41–50 in the pooled sample, so these results support a declining curve, with substantial uncertainty around any exact cutoff.

The eventual top 40 generated 89.9% of positive value, but knowing those names in advance would require hindsight. The preseason top 40 captured 58.8%. A player’s curve credit is the smoothed historical value at his frozen 2026 ESPN rank. Subtract the curve credit at the actual pick to measure acquisition surplus. This gives an early bargain more weight than a similarly sized late rank gap. It transfers a broad historical price curve onto expert ranks; it is not a fresh projection of that player’s 2026 points.

The balanced index now allocates its existing 20% draft-value budget equally to ESPN auction surplus and historical-curve surplus: 60% starting points, 20% coverage, 10% ESPN value, 10% curve value. The two value measures are related and are not treated as independent forecasts. Curve credits measure roster acquisition, including bench assets; they are never added to lineup projections or Elo-style power. K/D/ST are excluded, and unranked Beckham receives no curve credit. Known ranks beyond 140 use the last observed band’s value, without inventing a cutoff.

How the score works

The revised balanced index weights four within-league percentiles: 60% starting offensive projection, 20% mean percentage retained after one starter is removed, 10% ESPN salary-value surplus, and 10% historical-curve surplus. This splits the existing value allocation; it does not give two related price measures twice as much influence. For each input, the lowest of eight teams receives 0 and the highest 100; ties share their average rank. An index of 97 is not a 97% chance of winning. The Lab supplies starter-heavy (80/10/5/5), depth-heavy (40/40/10/10), value-heavy (35/15/25/25), previous-index (60/20/20/0), and historical-value (60/20/0/20) comparisons.

ESPN value surplus sums each offensive player’s salary value minus the source salary value at that overall pick number. It accounts for the different draft slots and avoids pretending that moving from rank 200 to 150 is worth the same as moving from 50 to 1. The only unranked offensive pick, Beckham, receives zero modeled salary value; his rank remains unknown. These values are scaled for a ten-team, $200 auction and are only a benchmark for this eight-team snake.

Historical-curve surplus applies the same acquisition calculation to a points-based rank-price curve derived from 2016–2025 public results and preseason ADP. Full assumptions, source mismatch, replacement definition, zero-production picks, smoothing, and tail handling appear in the value-curve section and its Lab. The published grade uses the complete ten-season two-QB proxy; selecting a different sample in the Lab is a sensitivity experiment.

Letters are analyst judgments: A-range drafts combine contender-level lineups with useful value and cover; B-range drafts have competitive lineups and identifiable repairs; C-range drafts have substantial acquisition or structural weaknesses. They are not obtained by converting the relative index into school percentages.

Projection selection, injuries, and replacement limits

Lineups maximize Clay’s published PPR points under the confirmed slots, with ESPN rank breaking point ties. Dividing the lineup total by 17 produces a common season-average scale, not a matchup forecast. The source is generally a 17-game baseline but explicitly models fewer games for some players. Known reduced-game projections are retained without another haircut. Bench players do not all score at once.

The injury test removes each offensive starter equally, then reoptimizes. Equal scenario weighting is not a claim that their injury risks are equal. The supplied news notes are selective, dated checks, not a complete medical model. Missing notes do not certify health. The one-pickup sensitivity adds the best projected undrafted player at the removed player’s position, with no bidding competition or roster-limit friction. It is an optimistic scenario, not a verified waiver recommendation.

The three-WR sensitivity adds a WR slot and leaves five bench spots out of the same 16-player roster. K and D/ST remain displayed. Their projected points, streaming matchups, and return-touchdown probabilities are not modeled, and no championship odds are claimed.

    What the chess-style number means

    Each lineup receives 1500 + 400 × log10(P / G), where P is its published projected point total and G is the geometric mean of all eight baseline totals in the selected lineup format. The baseline ratings average 1500. G stays fixed when an absence or bye is applied, so lost production remains visible in the ratings.

    A rating gap of D corresponds to a projected-point ratio of 10^(D / 400). The logistic expression used in US Chess’s standard expectancy formula (§4.2) therefore equals P₁ / (P₁ + P₂) here: a share of projected offense, not the likelihood of winning a fantasy matchup. No outcome history, weekly variance, player correlations, or new player-performance forecast enters this calculation. Small rating gaps are intentional; this league’s projected lineups are close.

    The scale is an analytical display choice, not a US Chess or ESPN fantasy model. It does not add information beyond the underlying points. For actual matchup odds, we would need independently validated weekly uncertainty estimates and the league’s full scoring settings.
